01 The House

A townhouse that keeps
its own hours.

We are not in the business of dinner. We are in the business of the two hours around it — the pause before the first pour, the plate that arrives without ceremony, the conversation that outlasts the candle.

Built in 1948 as a family home on 100 Feet Road, the house still carries its original terrazzo floors, its verandah and its mango tree. We added twelve tables, a marble bar and a pastry room. We took nothing else away.

Meher Kapadia

Founder & Executive Chef

02 The Kitchens

Four kitchens,
one table.

Each kitchen has its own chef, its own fire and its own grammar. They meet only at the pass — where every plate is finished by hand, under one light.

i. Bengaluru · Delhi · Lucknow Modern Indian Stone-ground masalas, slow fire, and a respect for the recipes we were given — rearranged, never reinvented. Six plates → ii. Normandy · Lyon French Technique Butter, stock and patience. Sauce work that takes three days and says nothing about itself. Five plates → iii. Kyoto · Kochi Japanese Restraint Fish flown in twice a week, cut at the counter. Three ingredients on the plate. Never four. Four plates → iv. 06 The Experience

Four rooms,
four moods.

The house opens gradually through the day — coffee at the counter, lunch on the verandah, dinner by candlelight, and the last pour in the courtyard.

01

The Chef's Table

Eight seats at the pass, one seating a night. Eleven courses, no menu printed — the kitchen cooks what arrived that morning. Four hours, wine paired.

₹7,500 per guest · Thursday to Saturday
02

Salon Privé

A green-walled room behind a single olive door, set for six to fourteen. Linen, candlesticks and a service team of one per three guests. Yours for the evening.

From ₹28,000 minimum spend · Private events
03

The Courtyard

Beneath the original mango tree, seven tables under festoon light. Open from six for apéritifs and small plates, until the candles burn out.

Walk-ins welcome · Weather permitting

07 The Chef

“A recipe is only a memory someone wrote down. My work is to remember it properly — and then to let the evening be more important than the food.”

Meher trained in Paris, cooked in Kyoto, and came home to Bengaluru in 2011 with a notebook of her grandmother's masalas and a stubborn belief that a restaurant should feel like a house, not a stage. She still tastes every sauce before service.
